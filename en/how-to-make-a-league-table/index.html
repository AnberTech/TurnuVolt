How to make a league table (standings)

A league table shows at a glance where every team stands. Here is which columns you need, how points are awarded and who goes ahead when teams finish level on points.

What columns does a league table have?

In a standard league table each team gets one row. The columns usually run like this: Pld (played), W (won), D (drawn), L (lost), GF (goals for), GA (goals against), GD (goal difference) and Pts (points). If your game is scored in points or sets instead of goals, you can record that unit in those columns. Two quick checks help you spot some inconsistencies. In every row, W + D + L must equal Pld. And across the whole table, total GF must equal total GA. If either check fails, review your match records and table math. Passing both, though, does not prove every score is right.

Points systems: win, draw, loss

How points are awarded is up to your league's own rules. In sports with draws, like soccer, the common system is 3 points for a win, 1 for a draw and 0 for a loss. In sports without draws, like basketball, a win is often worth 2 points. In volleyball the score itself matters: a common rule gives 3 points for a 3-0 or 3-1 win, 2 for a 3-2 win and 1 for a 2-3 loss. If you run your own tournament, announce the rules before the first game. Changing the points system mid-season can easily start an argument.

Tiebreakers: who ranks higher on equal points?

Tiebreakers vary from league to league, so check your competition's rules. Common ones are goal difference (goals for minus goals against), goals scored, head-to-head results between the tied teams and, as a last resort, drawing lots. Some leagues apply head-to-head before overall goal difference, others after it. When three or more teams are tied, many rulebooks build a mini table from only the games those teams played against each other. Writing down the order of tiebreakers in advance lowers the risk of end-of-season disputes.

Why keeping standings by hand is hard

Keeping a table on paper or in a spreadsheet is error-prone, even in a small league. After every result, two rows need to be recalculated at once: Pld, W, D, L, GF, GA, GD and Pts all have to be checked for both teams. One missed number can throw off the rankings, and it may take a while before anyone notices. Postponed games, teams with a bye in a league with an odd number of teams, and a home-and-away format that doubles the games add to the work. A reliable habit is to rebuild the table from all results now and then and compare, but by hand that takes time too.

Frequently asked questions

What does GD mean in a league table?

GD stands for goal difference: goals scored minus goals conceded. A team that scored 20 and conceded 12 has a GD of +8. It is one of the most common tiebreakers when teams are level on points, but which criterion comes first varies, so check your competition's rules.

What is head-to-head and how does it work?

Head-to-head compares tied teams using only the games they played against each other. Points and goal difference from those games are common criteria. Check your league's rules for which criteria apply, in what order, and whether head-to-head comes before or after overall goal difference.

How many points is a win or a draw?

Your league's rules decide. In sports like soccer the usual system is 3 points for a win, 1 for a draw and 0 for a loss. In sports without draws a win is often worth 2 points. Whatever system you choose for your own tournament, tell everyone before it starts.

How do standings work with an odd number of teams?

With an odd number of teams, one team has a bye each round and does not play. So mid-season, teams may show different numbers in the Pld column; by the end of the league everyone has played the same number of games. Keep games played in mind when you read the table.
